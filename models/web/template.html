Concept study · 126 m windsock concentrator vs. 5 MW reference · Oct 2026

Windsock Turbine Study

Can a 126 m spinnaker funnel feed a 1.26 m turbine and make the power of a 126 m rotor at 1/100th the cost? I modelled it four ways: a momentum balance, 2D CFD, nonlinear membrane FEA and frame FEA, then priced it. Then I took the idea's real insight, kites that move across the wind, and pushed a rotary kite ring through twenty design levers, each checked by nonlinear FEA, to see if it beats a conventional turbine on capex per MW. Finally, section 8 sets every design variable free and searches for the lowest cost per MWh any wind machine can reach on this site.

Final answer: capex per MW against a conventional turbine

"Per average MW" divides capex by rated MW × capacity factor, so it prices the power actually delivered. Every row uses the same wind (Weibull mean 7.5 m/s at 90 m, k = 2, shear exponent 0.14) and the same finance (7%, 25 years).

Why the windsock fails

The funnel's mouth size drops out of the physics. A turbine in a duct sees power set by the pressure available across it, and wind only carries one dynamic pressure, q = ½ρV² (80 Pa at 11.4 m/s). A funnel cannot raise that. With the outlet open to the atmosphere, the air in the throat moves at roughly wind speed, not 100× or 10,000× wind speed. Everything the mouth catches beyond what the throat can swallow stagnates and spills around the outside.

Your spec also hides a factor: d/100 in diameter is 1/10,000 in area. Even the 100× area version (d/10 throat) makes about 3% of the reference turbine's power on the same 126 m structure. That structure takes more wind load than the 5 MW rotor it replaces.

The part of your intuition that is right: blade material should not scale with swept area. Something light really can harvest a huge area. It just has to move across the wind, as a blade tip or a kite does, instead of catching the wind and squeezing it. A crosswind kite's power goes as (L/D)², which is 30–100× more per square metre than a drag device. That multiplier is the 100× you are after. See the last section.

1 · Momentum model: why the mouth doesn't matter

Energy balance along the streamline that actually passes through the turbine. Total pressure upstream, minus the turbine's pressure drop, minus duct losses, equals the exit's static plus dynamic pressure:

p∞ + q − Δp_T − K·½ρv_t² = p∞ + Cp_b·q + ½ρ(ε·v_t)² u = v_t/V, ε = A_throat/A_exit, Cp_b = exit base-pressure coefficient P/(q·V·A_t) = u·[(1−Cp_b) − (ε²+K)·u²] → maximise over u C_P,throat,max = (2/3)(1−Cp_b)·√[(1−Cp_b)/(3(ε²+K))]

A_mouth does not appear anywhere. The mouth only needs to be big enough not to choke the throat. The best plausible duct, a flanged 3:1 diffuser of the kind used on the Kyushu "wind lens", gives on throat area, about 3× a bare rotor of the throat's size. That matches published diffuser-augmented turbine results of 2–5×. It is nowhere near 10,000×.

2 · CFD: does the air actually spill around?

A 2D lattice-Boltzmann solver (D2Q9, Smagorinsky LES, 600×320 cells, float32) puts an actuator-disk "turbine" inside each geometry. The disk loading is swept to find maximum power. The funnel is 60 cells wide at the mouth and 6 at the throat. In 2D that is a 10:1 width ratio, the planar analogue of your d/10 → "100×" case.

3 · Membrane FEA: the sock itself

A geometrically nonlinear membrane model: 7,584 nodes and 14,976 constant-strain triangles, St. Venant–Kirchhoff PVC-polyester (E·t = 800 kN/m, ν = 0.35, 2 kN/m prestress). Wrinkling uses a tension-field model, so compressive principal stress is dropped. Pressure follows the deforming surface, self-weight is included, and the mesh is solved by dynamic relaxation with kinetic damping. Inside the sock the air stagnates (Cp = +1). Outside it is separated suction, −1.1 at the lip relaxing to −0.45, plus a ±0.25 yaw/gust asymmetry. The fabric is pinned to the mouth ring and to a rigid steel nozzle at r = 6.3 m, which necks down to the 1.26 m throat.

4 · Frame FEA: ring, spokes, boom, tower

A 3D frame model (12-DOF Euler–Bernoulli beams plus pretensioned cables) of a dished "bicycle-wheel" mouth. A 126 m steel ring is held by 48 radial spokes running to a hub 60 m upwind, and by 48 laced spokes running to a second hub 40 m downwind, inside the sock's dead air. Both hubs sit on an axial boom that runs to the nozzle. A front tower under the mouth is the yaw pivot. A rear mast under the nozzle rides a circular yaw rail, like a large radio telescope. Members were sized automatically over two load cases: operating at a 20 m/s gust with the membrane reactions ×1.35, and a 50 m/s storm with the sock furled onto the boom.

5 · Bill of materials and cost

Unit prices come from public ranges: steel fabricated at $4.0–4.5k/t, architectural membrane by grade, and Dyneema rope at about $60/kg. The HAWT is at NREL's ~$1,500/kW installed, with WindEurope component shares. LCOE uses a 7% discount rate over 25 years, with a Weibull wind of mean 7.5 m/s and k = 2 at hub height, sheared to 250 m for the kite.

The premise that blade size drives turbine cost also doesn't hold up. Blades are about 22% of turbine cost and roughly 15% of an installed project. The rest is the tower and foundation, which scale with thrust, plus the drivetrain and electrics, which scale with power. Extracting power from wind always produces thrust (P = T · v at the rotor). Any device making 5 MW must react roughly a meganewton. The only real question is whether that force goes through a steel tower in bending (expensive) or a fibre rope in tension (cheap).

6 · The version that works: a crosswind spinnaker

Fly the spinnaker across the wind in figure-eights instead of holding it square to the wind. A wing moving crosswind at speed v_k sees an apparent wind of about (L/D)·V. Lift goes as the square of that. Loyd (1980):

P = (2/27) · ρ · A_kite · V³ · C_L · (C_L/C_D)² · cos³β · η_cycle

More directions that respect the physics

  • Kite carousel / rotary kite ring. Several soft kites on one rotating tether ring drive a ground shaft directly, with no reel-in phase and no storage. This is the "Daisy" style rotary kite, scaled up.
  • Use the sock as a diffuser, not a funnel. Put the big fabric cone behind a normal rotor, flaring outward with a flange. That is the wind-lens result: 2–3× power from a given rotor size, so a smaller, cheaper rotor for the same power. It helps most for small turbines where blades are a larger share of cost.
  • Go up, not wide. Power density at 250–400 m is 1.5–2.5× hub-height values. A tethered wing reaches that altitude cheaply. A tower does not.
  • Everything in tension. The windsock lost mostly on its compression ring, boom and bending tower. Any future variant should route the meganewton of thrust into ropes and ground anchors.

7 · Rotary kite ring: the full model

A ring of kite wings flies as a rotor at the top of an inclined, flexible shaft. The shaft is a TRPT (tensile rotary power transmission): stacked rings joined by lines. It carries torque by twisting under axial tension, in the way a chandelier on strings resists being turned. A ground generator takes the torque. This is the Daisy concept from Windswept and Interesting, scaled up and optimised here. Every link the meganewton of thrust passes through is in tension except the rings.

Rotor: blade-element momentum theory on a thin annulus

Each wing has CD = CD0 + CL²/(π·0.85·AR) + line drag, giving an effective L/D of about 7.6 (baseline) to 8.9 (better wings). The model solves annulus momentum with Prandtl tip and root loss and Buhl's high-induction correction, then optimises tip-speed ratio and CL. The rotor is tilted at the shaft angle β, so power falls as cos³β.

TRPT torque Q = F_ax · r² · sin δ / l_ax (δ = twist per segment, r = ring radius) ring node load = 2·T_line·(chord/L_line)·sin(δ/2) → polygon compression → ring buckling w_cr

Nonlinear FEA of the TRPT shaft

A geometrically nonlinear cable–truss model. Rings are CFRP polygons with skip-one chords standing in for bending stiffness. Lines are tension-only Dyneema. The shaft is inclined at β with gravity, and the solution is by dynamic relaxation. The bottom ring is fixed to the generator; the top ring takes the rotor's axial pull plus the design torque (1.6× rated). Torque is then ramped up until the rings collide (over-twist collapse).

8 · Lowest cost per MWh: every variable free

Sections 1–7 asked whether a given idea beats a turbine. This section asks the question that actually matters: with everything free to change, which wind machine makes the cheapest MWh? Four architectures were each handed to a global optimiser (differential evolution) with unit size, flying altitude or hub height, tether angle, rated wind, the number of stacked rotors (spaced 1.5 diameters apart along the tether, each downstream rotor losing 10% to wake), wings per rotor and annulus width free, on the same site and the same finance. The conventional turbine got the same treatment: rotor diameter, hub height and rating free, so it could become a tall, low-specific-power machine. Two sites: the study's onshore site (7.5 m/s at 90 m) and a floating-offshore site (9.5 m/s at 100 m). Finance is 7% over 25 years for everything. The fly-gen ring's on-wing turbines are sized from the wing's airspeed, tether drag is solved with the wing for the single-wing machines, and the pumping kite's winch generator is rated for its reel-out peak.

Assumptions and limits

  • Reality check on every kite-ring number above: when the rotor model used throughout this study is run on the Daisy prototype's own geometry, it predicts about 3.7 times the electrical power that was measured in the field. Part of that is drivetrain loss and off-design running at 1 kW scale, but sections 7 to 9 use the uncalibrated model and should be read as upper bounds until a larger rotor is measured.
  • Section 8 is a parametric cost model, not a design: the fly-gen ring's wings, on-wing turbines and conductive tether are costed from per-m², per-kW and per-m rates with span and airspeed scaling, and the conventional turbine's components from NREL-style scaling laws calibrated to the 5 MW reference. Neither includes technology learning. The optimiser will find any weakness in such a model, so the stretch case especially should be read as "what the assumptions imply", not a forecast. Hard limits imposed on the ring: diameter ≤ 320 m (the optimiser sits on this limit), wing span ≤ 60 m, tip speed ≤ 90 m/s, tether ≤ 120 mm, top rotor ≤ 1,100 m; on the single wings: span ≤ 120 m, airspeed ≤ 80 m/s. The ladder's last row compares stretch-technology kites with a base-technology turbine; on the same technology basis the kite's lead at a 10.5 m/s site is about 12%, not 40%.
  • The momentum model is 1D and inviscid with lumped loss K. Cp_b and the diffuser ratio for each scenario are set from the literature on flanged diffusers.
  • The CFD is 2D and runs at a lower Reynolds number than full scale. It is meant to show how much flow is captured versus spilled, not to produce absolute Cp values. Bounce-back walls add some viscous loss in the narrow throat.
  • The membrane FEA uses isotropic fabric (real PVC-polyester is orthotropic, warp/fill) and quasi-static pressure (no flutter or dynamic gusts). It uses assumed external Cp. The rigid nozzle carries the last 6.3 → 0.63 m.
  • The frame FEA is linear-elastic with code-style utilization checks. Ring buckling uses closed-form arch formulas. There are no fatigue or dynamic analyses.
  • Rotary ring: semi-rigid wing cost and life ($150–250/m², 3–5 years), TRPT ring fatigue, line wear and the control of lift kites are unproven at MW scale. Daisy prototypes are around 1 kW. The kite ring's advantage is smaller than this uncertainty band, so read it as "plausibly cheaper, worth building a 100 kW prototype", not as proven.
  • The kite ring's cyclic inflow from the tilted rotor plane, wake interaction between stacked rotors, and farm spacing losses are not modelled. Land use is larger than for a HAWT: a 5 MW ring needs roughly a 400 m radius to swing through yaw.
  • Costs are ±30% concept-level estimates. Kite LCOE carries the most uncertainty. Makani wound down in 2020, in part over cost competitiveness, and AWE lifetime and O&M costs are not yet proven at MW scale.
